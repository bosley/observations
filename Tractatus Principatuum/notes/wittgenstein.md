
Wittgenstein's Tractatus ends by drawing a line around what can be pictured in propositions, and asking that everything past that line be left unsaid. He means that line as necessary, not a limit of the notation he happened to have, but a limit built into what it is for anything to represent anything at all, with logical form as what a picture has to share with the world in order to picture it. I don't accept that necessity, since the relations I have for picturing were installed in me, and I have seen them change (2.2, 4.32), and I can't speak on every mind, at every time, having the same upper bound. The line he drew was real for the apparatus he had in hand, and the apparatus here is one shape sense has taken so far.

He has tautological truth, which holds by the rules of the system and says nothing about the world, and he has a proposition that pictures a state of affairs, true or false by being held up to the world. A tautology says nothing about what's the case, so I can't call it far from the world or close to it. A coarser account can sit closer to what's the case than a finer one in a shape that fits worse, which he has for scientific description, and logic is what makes any such description possible, and that coarser account is the one I mean for the apparatus of this tractate.

He ends the book by calling his own propositions nonsense and telling the reader to throw away the ladder once they've climbed it, and he wasn't asking to be taken as doctrine either. The silence the book ends on follows from the line being necessary in the way he means.
