# Choosing the good

## Absolutism and relativism, read as causes

I fill the car with gas. I'm not doing it for a moral good, I need to get somewhere. I need to get somewhere because I'm working, and I'm working to make the income that supports my family. Asked enough times, the why ends at the people I'm acting for, so the fuel is a moral act, a small one, a few steps downstream of its reason.

I think most people collapse into one of two positions about the good, moral absolutism or moral relativism, and I don't think the dichotomy is false. The two are usually treated as conclusions, what a person ends up having after they've thought about ethics. I think they behave more like a cause. A mind is already acting from one of them, and when it's asked to explain itself, it backfills the position.

## The small act at the wrong depth

If I hyperfocused on the fuel I could end up an eco rights person, and that would at least be arriving at a position from the act. What I see more often runs in the other order. Someone identifies with a person already in a group, one that wants a lower carbon footprint, say. The message makes them feel good. Then they look at the small act of fuelling a car and see a big evil in it, which puts the cart before the horse.

The misreading takes one attribute of the act, the burning of fuel, brings it to high resolution, and drops the relations it sits in: the drive, the work, the family at the end of the chain. Calling the fuel evil is the same mistake as taking the putting down of a fork for a change of life. The fork and the change of life are both real acts, several steps apart, and the person has fixed on the one nearest to hand.

## How a mind gets built

Relativism, I think, is the default case of a human, and the reason is in how a mind is made.

A new mind arrives on the scene without a personality. There's no one in there yet. The family bootstraps it, with its vernacular, its rituals, its routines, and the child begins to imitate the people around it until an individual mind coalesces out of the imitation. I'd call the imitation competitive mimicry, roughly in Girard's sense, where positions are taken up from the nearest people and taken up without the reasons that produced them.

The parents are mostly not thinking of this as building anything. They're interfacing with the rest of society and with the children at once, and everything goes across: body language, speech, sarcasm, and social inferences on language the child can't yet pull apart. A household with strife between the parents is the case I keep in mind. Parent A repeats some pattern day after day without noticing it. Parent B gets frustrated by the pattern, isn't confrontational, never says anything to A directly, and starts barking at A in other ways. The child of A and B sees the barking. They don't see the pattern or the frustration that went unsaid, only how it implicates them, how it comes across to them, and they don't yet have the capacity for more. B has a blind spot of their own in not being aware of it, or not wanting the confrontation, and so does everyone else in the house.

A mind is made of forms, sets of relations, and what crosses from one mind to another is the structure of those relations. The experience stays with the person who had it. The child receives the bark as structure and fills it in with their own experience, which is how a frustration between two adults becomes something about the child. The same happens with every presupposition of the world. They come installed along with the identity, carried by language in the wide sense, what's said, what's done, and what's repeated, and the identity gets carved out of them.

Where a family has a dictated center, the Ten Commandments, a notion of the true will, a set of principles from an eastern religion, the child gets a center installed with the rest. Where there's no center, the mind is left to identify with those around it, and it adopts positions without knowing why. That's what I mean by relativism as the default.

Parenthood is the plainest case of a mind being built by other minds that mostly can't see what they're putting in. So, back to the fuel. The person who sees it as an evil is often carrying a position that went in this way, from the people around them, and nothing has asked them why.

## Automatic and active

As time goes on the built mind has to act. There are base needs, feeding itself. There are social pushes, go to college, say. Some driving force is at work in every mind, the kid's and everyone else's. In every moment a series of actions is available, and people often feel like slaves to the ones they take. I hear people say they have to do X, they have to do Y. Mostly that's the driving function, participating in society as a whole and meeting the body's needs, with forward movement demanded as time marches on.

In each of those moments a person can be in something like a bypass, an automatic mode, and a lot of people fall into it once they're comfortable. A new job is hard because everything in it is new. After a couple months there's a rhythm, a routine, and that's usually when people become productive, since they're participating in the structure without having to think about it. Once the structure is normal, attention to it drops.

Take the structure away and many people idle. I keep seeing it with people leaving the military. David Goggins said something like, when he first got out of the army he just wanted to eat donuts and be lazy, though I may be misphrasing him, it's a memory. People retire and get fat. Cops and people with tedious jobs get fat. If a person can sit around all day and not engage with the world, they often won't engage with it. Some people live that way as their baseline, and any human being can have it happen to them. I don't mean that as disparagement, it's what I see.

The self-actualizing material, the motivational speakers, embrace the suck, comes down to doing to yourself what the military does to a recruit in the first week. Old habits are broken down and a rigid new set of behaviors is set in. The personality survives and is molded to a new structure. The ego, the way you see yourself in this moment, gets destroyed and a new one installed, and then you insert into the daily routine things that keep causing you to change. That this has to be described at all is a little ridiculous, and it has to be because lives are comfortable enough now that someone can have a home and enough resources to idle by.

When those speakers say eat a salad, ride a bike instead of driving to work, they're hinting at a change of mindset. What gets heard is the behavior, the downstream effect, as if the salad were the change.

Being active, in the sense I care about, is an uncomfortable state. It gets discussed as critical thought, and sometimes misconstrued as critical thought alone. I think it's critical thought plus the application of core beliefs. Some people find, once they're in it, that they don't know what their core beliefs are, which can be a problem of communication or a problem of self-awareness.

## The easiest moral side

If relativism through social play is the default, and people tend to take the easy path and settle, the result is a person who believes they're moral because they took the easiest moral side available, from a worldview installed by their parents and their growing up. Not letting anything be challenged also means not challenging outright evil, sometimes. The usual cover is the lesser of two evils. I don't think anyone has to choose the lesser of two evils. You can just choose good.

The fuel is the same case again. Calling it evil lets a person feel empowered morally, active, because they're speaking about it, and they aren't thinking about it. They're looking at one downstream effect of being a moral person, a person whose actions are balanced across a whole life, hyperfocusing on that one thing, and getting upset. I see it apply to nearly everything. It's something like the sin of being human, the ability to distinguish without understanding.

To change your perspective on the gas, you'd need enough reason to go through the uncomfortable state, out of the flowing one people tend to prefer.

## Two people missing each other

Abortion is the clearest case I have of two people talking past each other, each from a position they may not be able to state.

One says abortion is wrong and should be illegal. The other says you can't tell a woman what to do with her body, which is a reasonable response. The usual reply is that it's also the body of a baby. From there the argument goes into trying to distinguish cells from a body from a being, and I think that's fruitless. Once the cells start dividing there's the potential for a human being, and that's where the anti-abortion position comes from. There is now the potential of a human, it's growing, and under normal circumstances it's born. They don't want the potential wasted, and they put that waste in the same category as killing an adult human.

The absurd move I've seen is calling the baby a parasite. That's definitively incorrect. The term, and being able to recite its definition, gives the speaker the social pleasure of appearing intelligent, and on the strength of that appearance they claim the upper hand: you look stupid now, so you're wrong. Then the conversation turns into attacks on the person, or on the religious background that people against abortion typically have. 

## What gets deferred to

So it comes down to moral relativism and moral absolutism, and how a person sees the problem or act in front of them. The religious say this matters because humans can't agree on anything. I think a relativist would grant that, in a sterile setting, and I think relativism is birthed out of that same disagreement, the multitude of perspectives. The theist answers that the moral centers are divinely inspired, an outside authority, so we live by them and apply them.

I think anyone could agree that if every human being on the planet were a Christian and tried their absolute best to live like Christ, the planet would be wonderful. Told this, a person in the modern era will often say, yeah, but I believe in science, the very 2008 militant atheist teenager perspective. It implies worship of the process of science itself, and the things morality bears on can't be quantified within science.

So a person with a default worldview installed says they believe in nothing except science, which means they defer to an authority, a sort of priest: those who publish papers and have enough favor in society to be lifted up and put in front of them. They derive what it means to be a good person from the psychological interplay between themselves and those figures, how they see themselves relating to people like that, and the activities they engage in. In the general case the average person isn't thinking about it. Their morality is drawn from a sea of data around no center, and a sea of data has no tool for what good is, outside of balancing numbers.

## The state

Balancing numbers is, I think, the root of why communism will never work. Its adherents don't specifically worship science. They worship the state, and that's evil.

A state will inevitably be a system for balancing numbers, equating the value of lives against resources, since that is to a degree the method of a state. In the communist case a hierarchy gets established every single time. I don't think a totally flat society is possible. I'd like to see someone try, and I won't take part. The account says it's about the worker, and it is, and then there are the people who manage the workers, which always happens, and because they're fewer in number, by the communist calculation they count as more valuable than the workers themselves.

People complain about the same thing now with CEOs making more than their fleet of developers. The answer given is that the CEO has the vision, the connections, a personal tie, and puts more in so gets more out. I think that's fair, capitalism fair, and anybody can be a CEO, so if you don't like working for one you can go become one.

I believe the founding fathers of the United States were geniuses, maybe even divinely inspired. I won't let that become a tie to the state. I think any state worship is doomed to end in failure. The state is to be tended, the way a farmer tends a flock. You love it in the sense that you want it to do well, and to be the best it can be for its own sake, and you don't worship it, and you don't put faith in it.

## The two hands

Everything is done with an active love, to some degree, unless someone wants to hurt themselves. Love of the self or love of another is about all anyone acts on. I think it's because the opposite is destruction, and the self-destructive don't tend to propagate, or their beliefs and thought structures don't, because of the harm mechanisms in our biology that keep us away from them.

There are people who want to hurt others and don't even derive joy from it. Maybe they did once, and then it became their flow, and they stopped thinking about it. That nonchalant evil is the worst I can think of, evil that was once mindfully chosen and is now done passively, and maybe forgotten.

In every instance a person can ask what the most love is they can do there. If it's love of the self, the betterment of the self, it sits in the left hand. If it's love of another, including love of a process you might not like and engage with out of love anyway, it sits in the right hand. By evaluating which polarity the love has and why it's there, and uncovering the presuppositions of the world that came installed, a person can make sure the love they put out, to the self or to the other, is the best love they can give.

Doing that, a person begins to operate with a more authentic awareness. If two people were both aware in that way and held differing beliefs, they could respectfully disagree, or insist on a fight to the death.
