Most of what has gone wrong for me, where it lasted, has been a communication error. Some of that communication is a person reading a process nobody encoded and pulling a pattern out of it. Geometry was a reading of that kind, and it became mathematics. You can read the state of a system you already have an account of and see whether a prediction holds, and you can read a system you know nothing about until a theory forms and then test the theory. Those readings sit beside the other kind, material a human wrote for another human. A thermometer can be read again in the morning and the number checked, and the sentence a sick person writes about the same night cannot: the first is a process, and the second stays inside the writer and the language.

Once a group shares symbols for a pattern both sides can see, and shares the rules for using them, a statement inside those bounds can be checked against the rules. Groups can symbolize one pattern differently, and the symbols remain language. What makes "true" operative is the agreement on process, inside a system. "Twelve times twelve is one hundred forty-four" can be recomputed by a second person, and "the contract was signed on the fourth" is just as plain. I mean history in that wider sense, any temporal record, a fact written on paper today included.

However empirical the writer tries to be, the record is how they saw it. Words about a social structure, a motive, or a sensation do not hand the thing over. A poem about a sunset can stir a similar feeling, and the reader does not have the sunset. Describe a function, double the number and add one, and the other person can run it and come away with the same definition and the same output. Memory may tie the function to a classroom or to a person. The math is the math, because the subject was the math. Photos and clippings can establish that the sailors came home after the war and that the families were on the docks. What it was to meet them after that kind of time is absent from the photos. Secondhand tellers can miss the truth with no intent to lie. They pass on what they understood. Enough writing can establish that people thought a thing happened, and the state of affairs can stay unread. A small fact, misreported in innocence, hardens into the received account: the contract was signed on the fifth, the note said the fourth, and the note is what remains.

The language inside the record is a second trap, intent aside, because the language moves. Medical Latin minimizes the error, a dead vocabulary with rules for how to represent the thing, and a remainder of drift stays in it. A living language fails in two shapes at once. "Unlockable" can mean there is no lock, or that the thing is locked and cannot be opened. "Biweekly" is built so it can be read twice in a week or once in two weeks. "We need to have a talk" carries a social weight the culture supplies, unwritten, and a reader in a thousand years has no way in. "Come here," said to a child, can be the arms or the punishment, and the hearer has to already know which. Lawyers took up "shall" to freeze an obligation in place, and a future-tense reading of the word survived the attempt. When the Iliad was current, nearness let the people who heard it understand the story in a way a later expert who has the words does not recover.

An utterance does not show whether the speaker has the language, is trying to be accurate inside it, or is speaking out of a mood, and the mood changes what gets said. When writing was costly, formal work kept the mood down, and the mood remained visible in the formal work. The average case is conversation. The errors are innocent, and they happen every day, with me as much as with anyone. Someone mistakes Wednesday for Thursday and briefs the day they intend to have, and the whole account is about the wrong day, so the other person spends the day on plans that belong to Thursday. Someone says the keys are in the truck. The keys are in the car. The other person misses the train. Two people live together, one irritation never named, and later they are at a bar, a few drinks in, and an unrelated remark detonates the stored irritation, a fight about the wrong thing and out of proportion. Or the irritation was a week with a coworker, never brought home, and the outburst is spent on a child for a noise.

People make people, and a person does not come with an instruction manual. The new mind is unfinished. It has to be bootstrapped by its parents and by the situation it is in, and in that infant state it is subject to the everyday talk of half-conscious people who are themselves in some irritation, and everyone has blind spots however hard they try. Where the house is turbulent and the child's sympathetic nervous system is set high, the first meeting with a phrase, an institution, or a concept is set by how that meeting was staged. Say a steeple goes past the car window, the father's hands tighten on the wheel, the dial gets moved off a hymn, and nothing is said about churches. The feeling was his and unfinished, and the grown person later treats it as part of the church. By adulthood there is an internal monologue, a set of forms for the world and for one's place in it, and an identity, and the whole structure was built in language through context that was lived and not understood. The holes are inappropriate definitions, or incorrect ones, and emotional weight on subjects that do not warrant it. Nations, religious structures, political institutions, familial institutions, any hierarchy the mind has wrapped its head around, can sit in those holes.

Language describes and traps forms and constructs at different scales, and their relation to a person or to a group. A construct is a loose cluster of attributions, something nebulous that a human can be seen participating in. The first contact and the contacts after it bind a feeling to the construct, and later the sight of it produces disgust. Frustration near the thing is enough for the child to infer the cause and copy the response. Mimicry is how minds learn, and households pass hates this way.

I see it with a cross on a neck, met with a seething hate. This country was founded on Christian values. Yale and Princeton began as denominational schools training preachers to go out and tame the wild, theology first, and then law and medicine. I read the hate as generational church hurt, aimed at membership in the institution. Old women who garden, collect donations, and give the money to the poor get despised by college students and called names.

The vitriol aimed at rabbinic Jews, and I mean the religion, gets called antisemitism, while that same person will say vile things about Christ, who was a Semite. Islam draws less of this public contempt. I read the history as a net negative, an onslaught of violence and destruction for the sake of ignorance and child marriage, and I attribute the lesser contempt to that reading, and I separate it from any given believer.

A parent very much against communism, and the child is raised against it. A house that hates Christianity, and the child hates Christianity. A house that hates black people, and the child hates black people. They do not have to say the hate. They have to be frustrated every time the thing is near, and the child picks up the cause of the distress and mimics it. A communist badge, a red flag, a word on a shirt, and the disgust is there before the wearer has acted.

A counter-identity aimed at a dominant tradition keeps using that tradition's pieces. Some people will say, as a blanket, that they do not like Abrahamic religion. I have met the range as the Church of Satan, an atheist Satanism that takes Satan as a symbol and is not a reverse Christianity, and as theistic Satanism, the case organized around Abraham. Systems of magick that use the Kabbalah or the Qabalah, the Gnostic Mass, and a lot of neo-pagan ritual are tuned as the inverse of what an Abrahamic tradition calls proper. Polytheist practice, in the people I have met, mirrors the refusal. Three traditions that old and that large leave little ground unused. The sharper thing, from people I have actually interfaced with, is that they were still using the parent tradition's pieces, answering the parent tradition rather than starting from something they want on its own. The answer shows up outside that religious cluster, in a family role lived as the refusal of a parent. The parent was the rule of the house. The son's house keeps the hour, the foods, the corrections, and the day that was set aside, each of them inverted. A politics lived as the refusal of a parent politics keeps a membership, an expulsion, and a day on the calendar.

What gets called racism now is often the ban on tying physical differences, bone structure included, to a race, the demand that people talk as if the bodies were the same, and I reject the ban. Real racism is the kind visible in the 1940s, a vile feeling stuck to the construct of a race, so that seeing the form produces the hate. That vitriol toward black Americans and Asian Americans and Hispanic Americans has not continued in the same way. A similar level of vitriol is aimed now at white Americans, the level people have for the church, including when the person is ordinary, or old. The older cases were a person seen as black, or Asian, or Hispanic. A flag on a porch draws that disgust with no race in the case.

The strife of the house is in that weight. Past the strife, the mind's outlook has pieces that were inferred rather than installed. I suspect the following layers are present, the lower required for the higher. When a novelty shows up in a higher layer, a lower layer can backfill a default on its own. Backfill can be innocuous, a saying completed from a half memory. Backfill is also how an emotional trauma gets installed, a feeling written into a layer.

The hate at the cross, and the hate at a race, already treats those as kinds of being. Church, God, the woman in the garden, the person of the race the house hates, are filed as kinds of thing, and the kind arrives with the feeling. That sorting is the ontological layer, how the mind categorizes the totality of experience into categories of being, and how those beings relate. An inquiry like why there is something rather than nothing unveils default ontological groupings the mind had backfilled and not thought out. Most people never consider being at all, and never need to, so this layer is backfilled often.

A house that hates a people has bad already written on the construct, and the child's disgust at the sight is that evaluation firing. This is the evaluative layer, the axiological one. What is real, good, bad, and indifferent is derived from the axioms of the mind, whether they were made or installed. Intrinsic values sit here, and patterns of indifference.

The student can give the ruling that the old women are contemptible, and give it as a moral fact, while the value under the ruling was never judged by him. That value was backfilled from the room. The rulings are the ethical layer, the moral etchings, concrete rulings cut from the evaluative layer.

The child copies the tightened hands in order to stay the child of that father. The student keeps the contempt because the status of the room asks it. The fight at the bar is a loyalty to a wound the other person was never told about. This is the interpersonal layer, the social one: attachment, reciprocity, status, conflict. It inherits the etchings and it informs them, and it makes loyalty and authority more salient.

Attention actually sits on the wrong Thursday, the keys, the remark, the cross, the face, the flag, and the outburst gets blamed on the object there. The operational layer is that everyday landscape, forms, objects, events, plans, the narrative of the self, through which the observer actually attends.

A judgment defended at the social layer or the operational one will often rest on an ontological or axiological commitment. A person is in the room with the women, or with the white stranger, or with the cross, and an opinion is required, and an account of what those people are, or of what the good is, appears in the mouth, the lower layer supplying it. On a quiet day the operation only finishes a sentence someone half heard.

One sentence, said to a child, can write several of these at once. A child asks why he has to share his toys. I fundamentally disagree with the answer, and I would still give it to a child. Sharing is caring. What gets installed is caring as something you do for the social aspect of life, an axiological good under that, one case where an ethic applies for a social reason, and with those an ontological picture of what the good is. "We don't talk about that" writes a category of things that are real and kept outside speech, a moral etching that silence is the act owed, and a social rule that loyalty to the house is keeping the silence. "Be nice" writes the good as the smooth surface between people, an etching against being the cause of friction, and a social rule that the comfort of the room outranks the thing in front of you.

Outside any religious conviction, once egg and sperm have mixed I see a human, and any intentional stopping of that development is murder. It is lesser in degree than killing an adult or a living child, and ontologically it is murder, because of what the thing is. Intent is required for the name. A new mother trips and falls down the stairs and the baby dies, and there was no intent to kill, and I do not call that murder.

In the West, as I take the rights talk, humans are given specific inalienable rights, bodily autonomy and freedom of choice, limited where it takes away the freedom of someone else. A woman is pregnant and does not want to be, and it is argued that it is her body and her choice. The reply I use is that it is her body and her choice, and another human is now in the case. Then the argument tries to decide when it is a human. We know it is not the sperm, and not the egg. Once they have mingled, the question is whether there is more than a clump of cells, and when there would be. One threshold people reach for is the crossing of the vaginal canal, the same shape as the claim that a person becomes a new nationality by crossing a border. Another is the first time it can feel pain. Those thresholds are the fight, and they are why the question of when it is a human brings out the worst in people and cuts every layer, the operational and social understanding of which opinion you are supposed to hold colliding with the evaluative understanding of the good and questioning the ontological outline of what a human is. If the body is toxic, or any other rare scene, it is irrelevant to the blanket of society. The left, as I see it, likes to build the fictitious scene, redefine the words, and win inside it as the underdog.

The ban on tying bone structure to a race presents as manners, a rule about which sentence may be said in public. Under the manner sits an evaluative good and bad about the saying, and under that an ontological commitment about what a race is, a kind of body, or a story that must not be given a body. The speakers can take themselves to be policing talk, and they get vicious once the kind itself is pressed.

People who demand that a man be called a woman operate almost entirely in the operational and social layers, derive their ethics from those layers, and by being centered there they question an ontological grouping most hold to be self-evident, what a woman is. They dissect sex and gender. Sex, they say, is the biological aspect, and gender the social aspect, and the split lets them reassign the social sense to an identity and appropriate the word. Mother is split the same way. One reading is the body that bore the child. The other is a social role, and that reading is put forward as the replacement, so a person who did not bear the child is to be spoken of as the mother.

If we can change the definitions of kinds at will, person is loose in the same way, and I have to question whether the people who claim the permission to swap a kind are even people, and perhaps they are not. Some people in that edge community believe they have the spirits of animals and do not believe they are people themselves. The edge does not write the rule for the group, and it does not settle the question. The culture has allowed this to continue because we want to be nice, and being nice isn't being loving. If we really cared about people, we would not indulge these fantasies.


I have the layers in that order, the lower required for the higher. Ontological, then evaluative, the axiological one, then ethical, the moral etchings, then interpersonal, the social one, then operational, where attention sits. A judgment made there rests on an ontological or axiological commitment, and a higher situation can backfill the lower layer. The record is read by a mind built earlier.

Theologians and philosophers tend to sit and stay near the ontological and the evaluative. They work the questions of those defining layers, and they look at whether a person is acting in accordance with what he stated there. Which layer do you tend to operate on?

When you structure yourself on the ontological and let that restructure the layer you operate on with the rest of the view, the change of mind is metanoia, a turning of the mind, not a swap of one opinion.
