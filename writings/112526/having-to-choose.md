# Having to Choose

Every person who can read this exists in some way. They cannot remember before they were born, and they cannot recall what lies ahead. A moment is a segment of time, perceived as a persistent now. The chain of those nows is the continuity a person identifies as the self.

Each now arrives in a context. The context may be a task at work, a duty in the home, or whatever one is drawn to when circumstances are relatively relaxed. Each of these is a filter over the now being experienced. The filter, together with the intentions a person has inside it, forms the set of decisions in front of them. Whether those decisions are made freely is irrelevant. In the work task, in the household duty, and in the looser hour, the experience of the individual is having to choose.

Agency follows from being able to understand the context the nows are arriving in, specific to the domain, well enough to see the actions available inside the perceived authority of the person there. HR, the lord's measure, uses a narrower test. On that test, a subject demonstrates agency when they take it upon themselves to work out a problem they saw coming. When the move is appropriate and benefits a formal hierarchy, the hierarchy calls it acting with agency. By this usage, agency is measured by its usefulness to the people who set the terms of the context.

People often ask what drives the whole succession by way of a large phrase, "what are you doing with your life," as if a solid end had been deeply considered. Most people do not have one. The succession is produced by the contexts already in place, by the decisions those contexts force, by the habits and constraints that shape which options appear, and by the larger arrangements that keep generating the next set of conditions. Changing a full set of circumstances takes a series of decisions across that succession. Each one is still local, made under the filter the present context supplies, and the cumulative result can shift the filters that arrive later.

The nows arrive either way. A person can notice the context the present one is arriving in, see the actions actually available inside the authority they have in that domain, and choose one while knowing the choice will become part of the filter on the next now. Intentionality is that treatment of the choice. The choice one is having to make is taken as something that can change the conditions the later nows arrive under, and a series of such choices is how a full set of circumstances can shift while each choice stays local.

The claims are put in plain words on purpose. Terms like meta-cognition, deterministic, and world model carry baggage from the shared language of the modern West, and the replacements used here are a picture of the world and standing in another's shoes. The aim is a series of choices at various scales, with the forcing functions that make the choices relevant set out where a priest of the body and a priest of the culture can both find them agreeable. An antagonism that would cause a controlled uproar cannot be manufactured. The course taken is to write the claims plainly and let them sink into time. To get through to anyone at present, the expression has to be a baseline, almost dead-simple or stupid way of stating simple truths.

Humans are distinct, in some way, from the other species on this planet. Language, technology, and dominance among other species have suited them to the planet's current conditions and put them in a league of their own compared with other species.

We are born, we age, and we die, and there is no ability to change these conditions. The medical industry and its knowledge base are substantial. Medicine buys time, and it cannot reasonably be counted on to deliver ideal immortality before a given person expires.

Before a life and after it, the span of time is deep enough to be unfathomable. Before us there are stories and goods that were produced. After us, enough time and change will pass that it will be as if we never existed, because the material record of the individual will be lost. We were not there, so we do not know what truly happened before we were born. We will not be there at the end.

At every scale of life a person is operating in, being alive forces choices, and not choosing is one of those choices. Everyone is subjected to this, and nobody opts out. It feels overtly obvious to state.

Free will may or may not obtain. Operationally, a person should assume that it does. Whether the next move is determined, and one is only along for the ride, is ultimately a nothing burger. In both cases the person is subjected to the choice, and responsibility has to be instilled for what one partakes in. To do otherwise would call into question one's ability to rule oneself.

Own interest and other interest sit on a gradient. The two can be described in ways that look opposed, and neither description has to be false. Everything a person does can be called selfish, meaning in their own interest, and a pessimist will push that into a particular heartache: nobody loves you for you, they love you for how you make them feel. There can be truth in that account. Loving someone for the feeling they produce is a large part of love, and the love remains authentic. Wanting the best for another, fully for the sake of the other, is a pure love. Wanting that best and taking enjoyment when they reach it is no less pure. Forcing what you think is best on another against their will can originate in love, and the act is a tainted love because it overrides a choice that was theirs.

People are not always drawn toward the whys under their behavior, the ones that come from the picture of the world they carry. Every human operates inside some internally modeled world and sees themselves in it. The form is likely unique, with similarities clustering into types, and however it works, all humans model the world to some degree. The models can differ enough that a swap of deep-rooted perspectives would seem alien. The experiment subjects the whole person to the full breadth and depth of the other life, days included. A recitation of facts is a separate procedure from that subjection.

Mrs. Maam is a music teacher in her late twenties, freshly out of college. She grew up in a middle-class American life of football games and trick-or-treating, light-hearted and sincere, and she carries a smile as part of her identity. Mr. Sir is also in his twenties. He grew up in Southeast Asia, and at twelve he watched political extremists murder his parents. He carries a rifle the way she carries a smile. Subject her whole person to his life and it would be fair to expect trauma, because a day that is ordinary for him can be gut-wrenching horror for her. Subject him to the love of a stable middle-American family and the question is what his response would be. He has already stood a test of trauma. A guilt or a conscience that could not have existed without the experiment might arrive with that family, and he might be traumatized by the arrival. A further difference in the same experiment is capacity. Give Mr. Sir an operating IQ of 70 and Mrs. Maam about 120, and his biology may be unable to replicate her understanding of what he is being put through. The device would then have to perform a cognitive correction, raising his reasoning for the duration and bringing it back down after. How he would handle the raised interval, and the return to an operating IQ of 70, is the question that difference adds.

Both are human. The differences run through genetics and through experience, and each still has a base observer they would call themself. What seems particular to humans is the ability to hold several perspectives in mind as if each were someone, or something, else. We can stand in another's shoes and hold a view that is not ours. Not every human does this unless the environment prompts them. The plain form of the point tells the reader to push themselves. Mrs. Maam's ordinary day and Mr. Sir's ordinary day are one pair a person would have to hold at the same time in order to do it.

We like to build systems of management, trade, and comfort, so as to have a home and to know that the people we care about are taken care of. Sports in education have been used to teach resiliency, accomplishment, and teamwork, lessons taught while a society was pursuing conquest and taming the wild. Some people online call the same arrangement bread and circus, to keep people happy. Keeping people happy is one function of a society. Treating it as the only reason for the circus is a narrow account. The circus is spectacle and awe, and it invites people to wonder and to explore domains of human experience that academia and the modern work-life tend to lack. Those who are academically inclined, and those who identify as such even though they are nothing of particular note, talk disparagingly about the circus and about those who take part in it. They are hypocrites. They extract the equivalent medicine from acts they would often not be at liberty to discuss, lest we see how deranged the acts are. Nothing speaks more true of a soul than that which a person derives enjoyment from. If that sentence produces a sense of judgment, the question to ask is what one is enjoying that one does not want to identify with.

A priest of the body is an authority figure inside an established hierarchy. The body can be any relevant formal hierarchy: a government, a business, a nation. A head of health who says to do x is one. A priest of the culture is the informal, unofficial authority, a trusted podcaster who says y, or who says x, but consider y. During cultural unrest, unofficial speakers make claims. On the group scale, facts are established democratically, by what the group comes to count as fact. Strife between body and culture comes from the degree of difference between that established fact and the ultimate truthful fact. Blood is often shed to change the category, in the gaining of independence and in the formalization of governing authority. What decides the formal against the informal, and where the authority comes from, are not given.
