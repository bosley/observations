There’s a moment that happens more often than I think people like to admit. Someone says something — at dinner, in a group chat, something passing at work — and you feel the room wait for you to either nod along or just let it go, maybe even push back. People tend not to push back often anymore, and when they do, it’s violent and often wrong. Anyway. You don’t have to be prepared to give a speech in one of these times, but sometimes we reflect on them later, sitting in the shower thinking I should’ve said this, I should’ve said that. You end up with a half-formed sense of whether they’re right, or of whether you have to make up your mind in public. Most people just fill that gap with whatever they have on hand: if they’ve watched the news that morning, some slogan, some fact they heard once, a feeling about the person talking despite what is being said.

Filling that gap is already an argument. An argument, as I’ll use it here, is a set of reasons, statements, and facts that support a position. Adopting that set — or failing to inspect the one already sitting on you — is the process. You can construct the set on purpose. You can also let one attach to you and grow there. Either way, a decision has been made.

Why we argue at all

We argue because positions can’t support themselves. Positions exist inside the individual and in groups of individuals, and leaving them unsupported is just another way of letting the nearest available tools, or the nearest available slogans, or whatever in the context you relate to first, do the supporting for you.

Interestingly enough, it reminds me of some religious literature: wherever two or more are gathered, I am there. The idea being that it’s the sharing of the higher-order concept between the two actors that makes the thing present between them. I’m not making an argument that God exists only when two or more people are together. What I’m saying is it’s like temperature: the movement of an individual particle itself does not temperature make, but a group of particles moving together has the emergent effect of temperature.

Arguments are pretty modest in what they can actually end up doing, but sometimes they can go to extremes. Whenever you make an argument, or have an argument attached to you through things that you watch or people that you talk to, it structures how you see the world — similar to how language itself constructs the mind. When someone has more words in their lexicon to identify feelings, moods, and situations, they can see a higher resolution of reality. And when you have bundles of arguments, or bundles of positions inside you, that you haven’t necessarily iterated through to clean up, refine, or intentionally create, what can end up happening is the world appears discordant. Things begin to not make sense because the tools you have to make sense of things are not working.

Some arguments, when held in minds, group together and also emerge a larger-order worldview that the holder of said worldview may not even be aware that they’re holding. We see a small version of this when college students first take a philosophy course and learn about reductio ad absurdum, and next thing you know they can’t stop putting it into practice. A new tool becomes a hammer. That habit — treating every claim as something to collapse — shows up well beyond the classroom.

So if you gather statements and facts around a position and ask whether that position is entitled to sit where it sits, they can show you the edges of a claim — either a passive claim that you’ve adopted or a claim that you’re trying to formulate and assert. No matter what facts, statements, and reasons we bundle together, they can’t force their way into reality to make reality comply. They cannot make a thing true that isn’t. Reasons can organize a position. They cannot replace care, and they cannot make you responsible for the thing the position is about.

Oftentimes I see people get this backwards, and I think it’s because people have an issue coming to the objective understanding of truth. I’ve even seen this in some people when they use the expression “well that’s your truth” and other such drivel. Some people treat arguments like they’re weapons that settle the world — meaning it builds the world — when at best a tool is something that simply settles a position inside of a specific context.

Having a position on anything means you should have thought about it. You should hold that position. You should have your own understanding of the truth of reality and build from it — your position — not allow the position to be built upon you. Something that reminds me of is the Stoics. I don’t remember who said it, but they basically said if you let the words of others drive your emotions, which drive you to act, you’re a slave to their words. It is through the mechanism of passive argumentation that this occurs.

It is through things like social anxieties and social pressures that individuals are forced to self-reflect and determine what set of facts and statements they allow themselves to operate within. What I mean here is sometimes people think the cost of appearing wrong, or the cost of being ostracized for holding a position backed by arguments, is too great to communicate and perpetuate those arguments.

What limits how far an argument can go is partly modulated by social pressures, but it’s also partly modulated by physical systems — things that don’t depend on outside actors directly. So it’s really the context the position lives in, and how much the holder actually cares, and what the facts they think are in play are even capable of doing.

You can construct something that looks like an airtight argument and still have it fail because you aimed it at the wrong boundary, or because the person holding it needed it to be true in a way that no amount of reason was going to touch — assuming, of course, it is sound reason. Those are different failures. One is a miss in the world. The other is a refusal in the holder. Either way it should be obvious, but somehow isn’t, that scope is not infinite. When a context looks infinite, treat that as a failure of the map, or as a refusal to accept a ground — not as proof that reason has no edge. Similar to how mathematics breaks down at specific singularities, then we change how we look at the problem and see that it was an error in mapping, not an infinity in the thing itself.

What an argument actually is

An argument is a set of reasons, statements, and facts that support some given position. That implies there is a position, which means there’s someone holding it, which means there’s a context in which an argument can even be made. A position is never free-floating. It sits inside a situation, against whatever the person counts as relevant to their self-image or interest, inside a larger — usually social — context.

Context is the boundary that decides what counts. It’s also the boundary of the validation process. If you can’t name the context, you can’t even determine what counts as a successful argument, because you do not know what the argument is being asked to do.

Finding that context is part of the work, and people often skip it. They start with the reasons they already like, or the facts they already have assumed, and they treat whatever frame they’re observing as if it was already settled. It hardly ever is. Two people can exchange a lot of sentences that look like disagreement and never actually share a context, which means they were never in the same argument to begin with. We see this a lot with people who argue right past each other. Someone’s mad at them because they misunderstood some claim made. Then the person misunderstood the retort because the context was so off, but they assumed they understood the context correctly, and now they’re off to the races arguing completely beside each other. In my experience, that’s often a function of wisdom held by the individuals — or rather the lack of it. Intellect plus time can teach an actor to notice when this is happening, name it, and get the conversation back onto one ground. It does not always.

The three ingredients

Reasons
The first question about arguments is whether the thinking behind it holds. Not whether it feels like thinking. Whether it actually gets from some set of considerations to a position without quietly swapping in a new destination halfway through.

Reasons, in their very nature, are part-of and smuggle-in worldviews. You cannot have a justified reason without a corresponding worldview, whether or not you’re aware of it. They imply assumptions about what a person is, what a good outcome looks like, what kind of thing is allowed to count as a meaningful signal as opposed to the backdrop of noise.

When considering logic and arguments, you can constrain the consideration of a reason to such an extent that the reason looks justified and good, but its surrounding implications, and its effect on the larger picture, can ultimately negate the premise for using the reason in the first place.

Then there’s consistency. Are the reasons within the set logically consistent with the scope of all reasons, statements, and facts that the argument claims to provide, or implicitly provides. I say implicitly because arguments always have their implications. Can inconsistency exist inside of the reasons without deteriorating the overall argument? I think yes, some, and the interesting question is how much. How much wiggle room is there? Local slack can exist inside a reason without sinking the whole case. A clash that breaks the named context, or that makes two cornerstone reasons unable to sit together, is a different thing. There are also boundaries inside the reasonings themselves, and those are not the same as the boundaries in the larger context.

Statements
Statements are the sentences that you actually put down, the things you actually communicate, and they always mean more and less than you intend. The question is whether there exist implications that would render the argument logically inconsistent with the proposed context.

What I mean by this is fairly simple, and people still trip on it constantly. You say a thing because it supports a position you want. The sentence, once said, also supports other positions you did not want, or contradicts a boundary you already claimed, or forces you into a corner that you later refuse to occupy or acknowledge. If you are not checking what else a sentence does, you are not checking the argument. You are checking the slogan. If those implications clash with the context you already named, the argument is already in trouble, even if the original sentence still sounds good in the room.

Facts
Are they actually facts? That has to be asked first, because a surprising amount of what gets marched into an argument is not. It is a feeling with a date attached, or a story that happened to someone.

Even when they are true, the next question is whether they belong here, such that their essence affects the other reasons and statements in a meaningful way with respect to the argument’s context. A true thing that does not touch the reasons or statements is not doing work. It is decoration at best, or masturbation at worst.

Unrelated facts get associated through unclear language and extreme emotion. Sometimes not even extreme emotion, but the culturally perceived ramifications of holding contrary facts. Someone will stick a fact in an argument because dropping it would cost them something socially, or because the language at play is nebulous enough that the fact feels adjacent, even when it isn’t. Slogans do this work all the time. “Love is love” is one version of it: a phrase elastic enough to treat unlike things as the same kind of thing, and then to treat disagreement as a failure of the word rather than a question about the cases. That sort of nebulous behavior can be used to argue for and against things that people would otherwise find morally reprehensible.

How far an argument can reach

The resolution of an argument I look at as a sort of interplay between breadth and depth. Breadth is how much ground the argument tries to cover. Depth is how far down it’s willing to go into the particular, into the actual mechanism of the thing. You can have a very wide argument that is an inch tall. You can have a very deep one that only works on the one case in front of you.

How much you care, and how much the thing touches you, changes how sharp the argument gets. This is not a moral observation, it is a mechanical one. Care modulates resolution. If it barely affects you, you will often stay at a slogan. If it cuts close — if it is your child, your work, your name, your sense of being a fool — the argument tends to get finer, or it tends to get hotter, and those are not the same improvement. Sometimes intensity of emotional heat is mistaken for depth.

There’s also a difference between an argument said out loud and one sitting unsaid in the background. Explicit arguments can be checked. Implicit ones still operate. They still have reasons and statements and facts. They just have not been asked to stand up. The implicit argument is still an argument. It is just one that has not yet been asked to stand up.

The three forces

When considering argumentation, I’ve noted a balance between ethos, pathos, and logos — meaning a balance between credibility, emotion, and logic. Credibility is the sense that the holder, or the source they’re leaning on, is entitled to have the authority to be believed. Emotion is the weather already moving through the person and the room before any reason has been offered. Logic is the actual structure of the getting from here to there, including whether more than one kind of structure is being used at once.

I think these deserve to be called pillars because you can’t actually find an argument in the wild that is missing any one of them. You can pretend you have a purely logical argument, but it is still being held by a person with a temperature, and is still being asked to be believed. You can stack titles until the room goes quiet, and you still have not replaced the need for the thinking to hold and the facts to belong. Take one away and you do not have a thinner argument. You have a different activity that is borrowing the name.

Pathos is always present. Unexamined identity-heat is how it stops being a pillar and becomes the whole weather. That is the distinction I mean to keep.

Unironically, I still need to create an argument for why credibility, emotion, and logic are the pillars of an argument itself. So we need to look at: is emotion relevant? Is logic always valid? And who determines credibility? Those questions are the outline of the next piece. A lot of the first pass at answering them is already in how arguments go wrong.

How arguments go wrong

Emotion

Emotion is entirely relevant. Unfortunately, it is a dynamical system that is emergent with each individual, but also has ramifications on their outward-facing self and their role in crowds. Certain pressures gather. Certain memories heat up. What we call a position may be, at least in part, the temporary organization of those inner forces into a pattern stable enough to argue. It does not stay an interior event. It erupts into the face, the tone, the willingness to hear a contrary fact, and then it shows up in crowds, where one person’s weather becomes a temperature of the room.

Oftentimes people are blinded to the objective truth within reality due to a hyper-emotional response that’s modulated by their own sense of identity. Identity turns up the heat until plain facts blur. Not because the facts stopped being facts, but because seeing them clearly would cost a person a picture of themselves that they are not prepared to lose. At that point, the argument is no longer mainly about reasons, statements, and facts. It is about keeping the storm in a shape the holder can still live inside.

Borrowed thinking

Within everyday argumentation it is often the case that in passive argumentation — when someone comes up with an opinion on something whether they’re intending to or not, and trying to reason for themselves how they feel, or what they just passively pick up — they don’t know that there are multiple fields of logic that they are naturally employing.

This is a passive pickup of opinions. You can arrive at a position by trying to think, and you can arrive at one by absorption, and a lot of people cannot tell you which they did. The absorbed position still comes with reasons attached. They were just not yours first. They were just in the ether. They were in your feed. They were in the mouth of someone you already liked or hated. This ignorance — not knowing more than one kind of logic is in play, and mixing them without noticing — produces a kind of pseudo-logical reason that looks like thinking from a distance. It poisons the process.

Borrowed trust

Credibility is something that is often looked at as simply a series of titles and achievements within some established hierarchy, and many times individuals will utilize a deference to authority that is ultimately unjustified.

Titles can be useful signals, but they are not proof. A signal that is correct a lot of times is still just a signal. It doesn’t mean that it itself is correct in the case in front of you. Authority inside the classical hierarchical system can point you towards someone who has done the work. It cannot do the work on their behalf. Neither can it do the work on yours. Giving in to authority too fast is an error, especially when that authority has not been justified for this context. A person can be genuinely accomplished in one domain and still be a dilettante in the one you are actually arguing about. The room will often not notice, because the signal was loud enough.

Even published rigor bends. In modern academic studies, we have concepts of publishing and holding ideas up to a specific amount of rigor. However, other concerns can modulate the quality and depth of rigor that scientists utilize in the pursuit of goals, as they are affected by the modulation of self-identity. Incentives, identity, enough to keep a program alive, the need to not be the person who is wrong in public — these things change how deep a person will actually go. The paper still comes out. The hierarchy still stamps it. And the argument still borrows the stamp as if the stamp is the validation.

A simple check

So then, when looking at constructing an argument, or the process of argumentation, treat reasons, statements, and facts as three piles.

Sort through each pile against itself. Do the reasonings hold with the other reasons. Do the statements imply things that would ultimately subvert the statements. Are the facts objective facts, and do they actually belong here inside the argument. Then check the piles against each other and against the context. The validation is the iteration across that, not the feeling that you already know.

This is the work that we all undergo, and your identity will try to steer it. Emotion will try to finish it early. Borrowed thinking will try to hand you a completed pile so you don’t have to sort. Borrowed trust will try to stamp the pile so you don’t have to actually look. The point is not to become some perfectly dispassionate machine. The point is that the piles are checkable, the context can be named, and once you have treated them that way, you can hold a position without the position holding you.
