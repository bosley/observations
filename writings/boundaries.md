There are boundaries in every dimension of life that you could liken to singularities. Places where the logic you’re using goes circular, or starts contradicting itself. The feelings at each stage you can still pick out are valid — but add them up and they’re naught.

When the lay consider the origins of the universe they employ whatever logics they have. Most people are not philosophers, mathematicians, or physicists. And I’d say those specialists are among the least in authority to speak on the origins of existence itself. That’s because all of them are working inside the subset of the universe that happens to be perceptible and understandable to us. The systems we’ve made can uncover real insight about particular forms and processes. The notion that you understand the whole picture through something aimed at a bottom corner of the frame is absurd.

The explanations of how the world got here are vast. None of them will ever be validatable. Every statement we make about origins is a story. The specialist in the corner of the frame can parade their achievements and conclude a static universe only to later be disproven by a Catholic priest who earnestly wants to study the world. No matter what we tell ourselves about what we think we know, the Truth of reality could be veiled behind our own ignorance.

That doesn’t mean you get to opt-out of suspecting an explanation. If you don’t come to some understanding that makes enough sense that you can actually construct a worldview, the worldview you already adopted will form the implied understanding for you. Origin without consideration. A cultural or socially acquired belief, left unexamined, informing what you believe about the origins of everything.

Most of the lay aren't aware of this state of affairs. The adopted view of modern atheism, or modern reductionism, agnostic or not, often implies the spontaneous miracle of nothing becoming something. They permit it because it’s unfalsifiable, which makes it akin to a hollow god. A miracle without a miracle performer.

If it can’t be validated, you still have to construct your own narrative, and that narrative has to contend with something versus nothing. The lay have to consider that if they want a serious worldview they can derive ethics from. Unless that work is done, the opinions that stem from it — the downstream ones — aren’t grounded in anything they can center on and refine. Without that center their opinion holds no authority with respect to oughts, because those oughts can’t be grounded in a narrative-backed reasoning.

The assumption of “the first miracle” is no less valid than any other origin story. It does bring with it a reality in which miracles are possible, and then other miracles aren’t automatically off the table. Without considering the ramifications of your own origin story, you stay open to argumentative traps. Those traps can expose the singularities in your existential narrative and make a stance look as if it has been undermined. If you do not know those edges, or how to contend with the limits you have imposed on yourself, misunderstanding and intentional misdirection can slide a misconception into place with almost no friction.

Watch an orator do it. Someone in the room says you don’t get something from nothing, that a cup doesn’t just show up on the counter. The orator smiles and talks about particles popping in and out of a vacuum, as if the physics classroom already settled the kitchen. Soft laugh. The first person looks like they never opened a book. What got swapped is the word nothing. Same word, two uses. A vacuum is a something. It has laws and properties; the popping happens in it. The crowd treats that as a reply. The claim about nothing is still sitting there. A red herring is that move in miniature — a nearby fact that still sounds like the subject, aimed at how the room will take the argument instead of at the argument.

If you know where you stand from the root of something versus nothing, that sort of thing gets easy to hear. Every opinion you walk around with is some mix of facts, feelings, and ideals. The facts aren't something that need to be identified. They show up already spoken for, in the way you already use the words. Near a singularity — those places where your logic goes circular or starts eating itself — the feelings cloud first. Information that would have been ordinary becomes a threat or a relief depending on what the story needs it to be.

When the facts put a scene in front of you that contends with an ideal, the scene itself is not what sets how hard you feel it. Your notion of the reason for the scene modulates the amplitude. Why it's happening. What kind of thing this is. You can watch it in the open: a dichotomy of principalities, two camps on a political dipole, each with its own hierarchy of subgroups, same video of the same incident. They don't come away with two mild readings. They come away at opposite extremes. They can point at the same frames and still not mean the same thing by them. They're fighting about what the scene counts as, and the feelings scale to that.

Without knowing the root of the ideals, you can’t even tell which of those you’re doing. You think you’re looking at the facts. You’re looking at the use you’ve already given them.

Once you’ve written your origin story the real work of application comes in. Within the boundaries that you find acceptable in the likelyhood of being the cornerstone questions for justifying authenticiy of opinion.
