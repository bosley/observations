Everything is hard to sort for truth. Most of it is rabbit holes: histories, rule systems, mental drills that train people to handle abstract relations. Those relations are usually grounded in logic or geometry, which is to say in math and the records people have left of it.

Mathematics is the one form of communication you can check by redoing the work yourself. You do not have to take the other person’s word. The notations are still taught in language and still carry conventions, so they can be ambiguous. sin²x can mean (sin x)² or sin(sin x) depending on what you were taught. Proofs have been published and later found wrong. People argue about whether the underlying logics are discovered or invented, but you do not need to settle that to check whether a given proof holds. You can also apply a model to something it does not actually fit; working the relations again is how you catch that. History, testimony, and reports of emotion cannot be checked the same way. Where other records exist you can set them beside the claim and compare, but the events themselves do not run again.

Knowing arithmetic does not tell you what it is like to hold a newborn. Mapping patterns in the universe does not give you the things social life and the transmission of feeling require. A proof hands you relations you can verify without having lived the situation they describe. A sentence about holding a newborn also hands you relations, but the feeling is not in the sentence. If you have never held one, you read the sentence through whatever else you have experienced.

History—the written record people have produced in many languages—is full of distortion of more than one kind. A lie, as I am using the word, is the deliberate recording of something false. That is one kind. The others do not require anyone deciding to deceive: second-hand reports written down, transcription mistakes, translation problems, shifts in everyday language. Those are noise. It is difficult to know what in the record is solid. Distortion does not wipe every usable relation out of a record. When the same relation shows up in sources that do not appear to have copied each other, that agreement is evidence for it. When they conflict, the conflict is evidence that distortion happened in at least one of them. This is a weaker check than reworking a proof, because the original events cannot be rerun. A distorted record can still contain fact. Distance in time makes it worse, but the problem is not limited to the distant past. A document a week old can use a phrase whose ordinary meaning has shifted because of something that happened on social media; readers can then treat it as propaganda when that was never the intent.

When I say “history” here I mean any set of symbols meant to carry information for a later mind. This document is becoming history as I write it. That is broader than the usual sense of the word, though it includes the usual sense. Anything written or recorded for a mind to read or interact with is committed to history the moment it is made.

Language is those symbols, and it does not begin at the record. A mind is shaped in language before it can examine the language. Speech, gesture, and repeated behavior carry assumptions about the world, about selves, and about what counts as real. Those assumptions are pressed into the developing person through contact with other people. A new mind arrives without a ready-made personality. Family, local speech, ritual, and routine supply the first positions; the child takes them up without the reasons that produced them. The identity it treats as its own is built from forms it did not choose.

Words used even slightly off can hand different objects to the people who receive them. The same word can do that if the recipient does not have the intended sense of it. Errors enter both when something is sent and when it is taken up. While a mind is still learning by imitation, those errors can settle into complex misunderstandings that function as near-truths. A near-truth can be used in ordinary talk without forcing a correction, so the mind then applies it to the next thing it hears.

“The door is unlockable.” Does that mean the door cannot be locked, or that it is locked and we cannot unlock it? Most of the time context settles it. But if the phrase is first met while the mind is still learning by imitation, and there is no second account to check against, the misunderstanding can stay. “We need to have a talk” is a clearer case of the same problem. One person can learn the phrase from people who use it to mean “I want information” or “let’s fix this.” Another can learn it from people who use it to mean “you are in trouble” or “explain yourself.” Both can hear clear sentences containing the phrase and still be talking about different things, because each is reading later sentences through the use they first learned. The later sentences do not correct it; they get interpreted by the earlier form.

Not all of these settled misunderstandings are equally open to later correction. Some arrive so early and shape so much of what the mind can notice that ordinary conversation never reaches them. Some are local misreadings of a word or phrase. Others set the way the mind groups and organizes whatever it meets, so that later corrections can only operate inside those groupings.

I have been treating these as rough layers. The inmost layer is ontological: the forms by which a mind organizes what it meets so that any moment of experience can be made sense of at all. These decide what counts as a thing, what counts as the same thing continuing, what counts as a change, and which kinds of relation are even available to be noticed. A person’s answer to “why is there something rather than nothing,” or “when does human life begin,” or “what counts as a good act,” sits in this layer whether or not they have ever asked the questions on purpose. The answer can be one they worked out, or one that was never stated but was implied by other things they heard and then installed by inference. Either way it sets the character of the attention that goes out from there.

Once those organizing forms are in place there is a further layer for what counts as real, or good, or worth attending to. Whether contingency or necessity is the basic case, and whether a thing has worth of its own or only the worth someone assigns it, sit here. These work the same way the reading of “we need to have a talk” worked: learned, then applied to the next thing.

Out from that are the moral forms: harm, fairness, authority, and the rest of that family. Whether the mind treats those as applying only to the people immediately around it or more broadly depends on what the earlier layers already set. A mind with the same deeper forms can still treat “avoid harm” and “keep loyalty” as the relevant demands; those two will not always point the same direction.

The next layer is other people: attachment, standing, what is owed, fighting, care. The “we need to have a talk” example lives here. The sentences were clear; what each mind had learned to put under the phrase was not. Each of those readings was already shaped by whatever sat further in.

Outermost are the everyday forms the mind actually uses when it attends to something. This is where most of the rabbit holes are—studies of history, mental exercises, the rules of shared systems. Most of what gets called learning happens here. Which rabbit holes look worth going down is already decided by forms further in.

If the inmost layer is never examined, the mind fills it from the people around it and the way they were already going on. That was never an answer to why there is something rather than nothing, or when human life begins, or what constitutes a good act. Later a dilemma appears among the moral forms, and the conflict is often the first time the mind notices a presupposition it had been using. Sometimes the attention stays on the ethical situation while the conclusion is supplied by what a group identity requires; that requirement carries an implication about one of the deeper questions even if no one states it.

People go forward with these defaults. They do not come with a built-in procedure for checking truth the way mathematical systems can be checked, by reworking the relations and seeing whether they hold. Checking the deeper layers is left to the person who decides to do it, by going through the relevant experiences and seeing what the forms actually do when they are pressed.
