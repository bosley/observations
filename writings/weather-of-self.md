Recently I was considering how ancient stories of angels, demons, and other spiritual forces were an abstract notion for a “temperature of a culture” and how the loss of utilizing these analogies left us worse off.

Without being able or willing to use this language to help discuss and intuit larger patterns present in the dynamical systems of a culture we seem to have lost -- for a period of time — the ability to diagnose and resolve emergent issues. It wasn’t until the sociologically inclined researchers defined a new set of terminology that we were able to begin pointing at these things again without sounding like we had regressed back into superstition.

In that sense, it feels less like modern research discovered an entirely new category of reality and more like it slowly caught up with older intuitions. We stopped saying a people had fallen under the dominion of some spirit, and started saying they were responding to incentives, participating in feedback loops, being shaped by social contagion, group dynamics, mimetic desire, or whatever other acceptable phrase fit the room.

The same seems to be true at the level of the self. A person is also a dynamical system, though we usually pretend otherwise. We talk as if there is one clean “I” sitting behind the eyes making choices, but most of the time the self is a mess of competing patterns, impulses, memories, wounds, ideals, appetites, and inherited images.

This is where Jung becomes useful. His language around complexes, archetypes, the shadow, and individuation seems like another attempt to preserve a way of talking about patterns that modern language struggles to hold. A complex, for example, is not so different from one of these older ideas of a spirit or principality. It is a charged pattern inside the self that can seize perception and behavior before the conscious mind has time to explain what happened.

Someone can be “possessed” by resentment, shame, lust, ambition, fear, the need to be innocent, the need to be admired, or the need to punish. Modern psychology may give us cleaner terminology for this, but the older language often captures the lived experience more honestly. It feels less like having a thought and more like being taken over by a pattern.

This brings Hume to mind. When Hume went looking inward for the “I,” he famously could not find it. He found perceptions, sensations, desires, pains, pleasures, memories, and impressions, but never some stable self standing behind them all. The self, for him, looked less like a single object and more like a bundle of changing experiences held together by memory, habit, and imagination.

We speak as if the self is a clean sovereign center, but when inspected closely it often looks more like a weather system. Certain pressures gather. Certain memories heat up. Certain desires pull in one direction while fear pulls in another. What we call “I” may be, at least in part, the temporary organization of these inner forces into a pattern stable enough to narrate.

When we consider the self as a dynamical set of subsystems in constant movement, it is easy to see how most of us end up blind to the mechanisms that direct our actions day to day, and month to month. Often it takes a fixed external observer to map out the edges of the various storms and sources of pressure differentials inside us. They can offer us a sense of orientation, and sometimes peace, because it is nearly impossible to see the totality of a system while you are inside it, navigating the seas affected by it.

Another means of finding orientation can be found through embracing traditional systems that modernity has attempted to shuck off, just as it attempted to remove the descriptive language that once helped us name these forces in the first place.

Religious traditions are not only collections of claims about the supernatural. They are also inherited systems for noticing, naming, and ordering the movements of the self. Confession, communion, prayer, fasting, liturgy, scripture, meditation, and communal worship all function as repeated interfaces with the inner life. They help give shape to the nebulous and atmospheric so that we can properly identify and confront them through the stories of shared lived experiences that span thousands of years.

Modernity thought it was freeing us from superstition, but in many cases it also stripped away the maps by which people had learned to navigate themselves.

And those maps hold no matter which tradition you reach for. The value of confession, prayer, fasting, or liturgy does not rest on first settling every metaphysical argument about which religion is correct. These are navigational technologies before they are anything else -- inherited instruments for reading the weather inside a person and the weather inside a people. Treating them as nothing but discredited claims about the supernatural is like throwing out the compass because you have decided you no longer believe in north.

What we got in exchange was not clarity. It was a sterile landscape where the only permitted language is the measured and the published, and where any deep conviction is met with the quiet suggestion that you should feel a little silly for holding it. But the embarrassment itself is the symptom. We have trained people to flinch at the very vocabulary that once let them name what was moving through them, and then we act surprised when they cannot say what is wrong.

I do not think the disorientation we see around us is random, and I do not think it is only economics or politics. A self that cannot name its own storms gets steered by them. A culture that has forbidden the old language for its own patterns stops being able to see, let alone resist, the things that are acting back on it. The slide is what happens to any dynamical system — a person or a civilization — once it has been told it is no longer allowed to take its own interior seriously.

So perhaps the way back is simpler and less embarrassing than it sounds. We can stop feeling silly for having convictions. We can pick the maps back up, not as a regression into superstition, but as a recovery of instruments we were never wise to discard. The temperature of a self, like the temperature of a culture, does not go away when we refuse to name it. It only becomes something we can no longer read.
